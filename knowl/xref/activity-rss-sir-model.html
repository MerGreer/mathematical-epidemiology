The remote island of Tristan da Cunha has experienced several outbreaks of the common cold that have been linked to ships arriving at the island. A 1971 paper
 1 
“A mathematical model of common-cold epidemics on Tristan da Cunha” by B.J. Hammond and D.A.J. Tyrrell, published in the Journal of Hygiene
shares multiple datasets for outbreaks in the 1960s and models these outbreaks with a model related to, but different from, the SIR model we have been studying. These datasets helpfully include plots showing how many people are Infectious each day throughout the outbreak.

Answer.
  1. We enter model equations in line 20. We set the value of \(\gamma\) in line 24 and multiple possible values of \(\beta\) in line 35. We set the initial values \(S(0)\text{,}\) \(I(0)\text{,}\) and \(R(0)\) in line 26. We plot all three curves \(S(t)\text{,}\) \(I(t)\text{,}\) and \(R(t)\) in lines 59, 60, and 61.
    🔗
    🔗
  2. The first graph shows curves \(S(t)\text{,}\) \(I(t)\text{,}\) and \(R(t)\) along with the data from Tristan da Cunha. This lets us see the overall progress of the model. The second graph shows just \(I(t)\) and the data from Tristan da Cunha. This can make it easier to compare \(I(t)\) with the dataset because the scale of the graph is determined by just these two plots, meaning the vertical axis goes only as high as needed for \(I(t)\) and the dataset. When we include \(S(t)\) and \(R(t)\) the vertical axis sometimes extends much higher, and this scale can make it difficult to see the details of \(I(t)\) and the dataset.
    🔗
    For how well \(I(t)\) fits the dataset: in the default simulation, the dataset peaks sooner and higher than the model’s simulation graph for \(I(t)\text{.}\) This may not feel like a very good fit, and it may cause us to think about other combinations of \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) that can do better.
